\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Most of what ML-DSA adds to a C2PA credential sits in its certificates, not
its signature. An ML-DSA-65 manifest is \NvsESDSAsixtyfive{} times a
matched ES256 manifest, \NdeltaESDSAsixtyfive\,kB more per signer, and
certificates are \NchainShareManDSAsixtyfive\% of it; a timestamp brings a
second chain of the same size. On the production files we collected that is a
median \NcpqAddMedkB\,kB, about one per cent of the median file, and more
than a tenth for the small images that services sign at volume, which make up
most of the quarter of our sample that grows that much. Time is not the obstacle on application processors, and on
microcontrollers the carriage form sets the memory a verifier needs and the
energy its radio spends.

Most of that cost can be recovered inside the specification as it stands.
Because the C2PA trust lists anchor issuing CAs, a signer and a time-stamping
authority whose issuers are listed can each carry their leaf alone, which the
reference implementation accepts and which takes \NtsBothLeafPct\% off a
timestamped manifest and halves each path a
validator checks, at the price of a trust list that changes more often. A
verifier that caches validated signers must key the cache on the trust list,
the policy and the revocation status it relied on, or it keeps accepting what
it should reject.

The migration also tests the validators. Exercising the reference
implementation against post-quantum and classical credentials showed it
accepting time-stamping and OCSP-signing certificates as claim signers and invalidating a
credential over a timestamp it should ignore, and showed revocation stapled in
a CA-signed OCSP response, which RFC~6960 allows and C2PA excludes, going
unheeded. A credential is
quantum-resistant only when its signer, its issuers, its time-stamping
authority and its revocation evidence all are, so the order of migration
matters as much as its cost, and both are now measurable.
